**On the Curvature of Space**

By **A. Friedman** in Petersburg.

With one figure. (Received 29 June 1922.)

**§ 1.** 1. In their well-known works on general cosmological questions, Einstein¹) and
de Sitter²) arrive at two possible types of universe. Einstein obtains the so-called
cylindrical world, in which space³) has a constant curvature, independent of time, the radius
of curvature being connected with the total mass of the matter present in space. De Sitter
obtains a spherical world, in which not only space but also the world can in a certain sense
be regarded as a world of constant curvature⁴). Both Einstein and de Sitter make certain
assumptions about the matter tensor, which correspond to the incoherence of matter and to its
relative rest, i.e. the velocity of matter is assumed to be sufficiently small compared with
the fundamental velocity⁵), the velocity of light.

The aim of this note is, first, to derive the cylindrical and the spherical world (as special
cases) from some general assumptions, and second, to prove the possibility of a world whose
spatial curvature is constant with respect to the three coordinates regarded as space
coordinates, and depends on time, i.e. on the fourth coordinate, the time coordinate. In its
other properties this new type is an analogue of Einstein's cylindrical world.

2. The assumptions on which we base our considerations fall into two classes. The first class
contains assumptions which coincide with the assumptions of Einstein and de Sitter;

²) de Sitter, On Einstein's theory of gravitation and its astronomical consequences. Monthly

³) By "space" we here mean a space described by a manifold of three dimensions; the "world"
corresponds to a manifold of four dimensions.

⁴) Klein, Über die Integralform der Erhaltungssätze und die Theorie der räumlich-geschlossenen

⁵) See this name in Eddington, in his book *Espace, Temps et Gravitation*, 2ᵉ Partie, p. 10.

they concern the equations satisfied by the gravitational potentials, and the state and
motion of matter. The second class contains assumptions about the general, so to speak
geometric, character of the world; Einstein's cylindrical world and also de Sitter's
spherical world follow from our hypothesis as special cases.

The assumptions of the first class are the following:

1. The gravitational potentials satisfy Einstein's system of equations with the cosmological
term, which one may also set equal to zero:

$$R_{ik} - \tfrac12 g_{ik}\overline{R} + \lambda g_{ik} = -\varkappa T_{ik} \quad (i, k = 1, 2, 3, 4), \tag{A}$$

here the $g_{ik}$ are the gravitational potentials, $T_{ik}$ the matter tensor, $\varkappa$ a
constant, $\overline{R} = g^{ik}R_{ik}$; $R_{ik}$ is determined by the equations

$$R_{ik} = \frac{\partial^2 \lg\sqrt{g}}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_k}
- \frac{\partial \lg\sqrt{g}}{\partial x_\sigma}\begin{Bmatrix} ik \\ \sigma \end{Bmatrix}
- \frac{\partial}{\partial x_\sigma}\begin{Bmatrix} ik \\ \sigma \end{Bmatrix}
+ \begin{Bmatrix} i\alpha \\ \sigma \end{Bmatrix}\begin{Bmatrix} k\sigma \\ \alpha \end{Bmatrix}, \tag{B}$$

where the $x_i$ $(i = 1, 2, 3, 4)$ are the world coordinates, and
$\begin{Bmatrix} ik \\ l \end{Bmatrix}$ the Christoffel symbols of the second kind¹).

2. Matter is incoherent and in relative rest; or, less strictly expressed, the relative
velocities of matter are vanishingly small compared with the velocity of light. As a
consequence of these assumptions the matter tensor is given by the equations

$$T_{ik} = 0 \ \text{ for } i \text{ and } k \text{ not } = 4, \qquad T_{44} = c^2 \varrho\, g_{44}, \tag{C}$$

here $\varrho$ is the density of matter and $c$ the fundamental velocity; moreover, the world
coordinates are divided into three space coordinates $x_1, x_2, x_3$ and the time coordinate
$x_4$.

3. The assumptions of the second class are the following:

I. After singling out the three space coordinates $x_1, x_2, x_3$ we have a space of constant
curvature, which however may depend on $x_4$, the time coordinate. The interval²) $ds$,
determined by $ds^2 = g_{ik}\,dx_i\,dx_k$, can be brought into the following form by
introducing suitable space coordinates:

$$ds^2 = R^2\left(dx_1^2 + \sin^2 x_1\,dx_2^2 + \sin^2 x_1 \sin^2 x_2\,dx_3^2\right)
+ 2g_{14}\,dx_1\,dx_4 + 2g_{24}\,dx_2\,dx_4 + 2g_{34}\,dx_3\,dx_4 + g_{44}\,dx_4^2.$$

---

¹) Our sign of $R_{ik}$ and of $\overline{R}$ differs from the usual one.

²) See e.g. Eddington, *Espace, Temps et Gravitation*, 2ᵉ Partie. Paris 1921.

[p. 379]

Here $R$ depends only on $x_4$; $R$ is proportional to the radius of curvature of space, which
may therefore vary with time.

2. In the expression for $ds^2$, $g_{14}, g_{24}, g_{34}$ can be made to vanish by a suitable
choice of the time coordinate, or, briefly, time is orthogonal to space. For this second
assumption, it seems to me, no physical or philosophical reasons can be given; it serves
solely to simplify the calculations. It should also be noted that the worlds of Einstein and
de Sitter are contained in our assumptions as special cases.

By assumptions 1 and 2, $ds^2$ can be brought into the form

$$ds^2 = R^2\left(dx_1^2 + \sin^2 x_1\,dx_2^2 + \sin^2 x_1 \sin^2 x_2\,dx_3^2\right) + M^2\,dx_4^2, \tag{D}$$

where $R$ is a function of $x_4$, and $M$ in the general case depends on all four world
coordinates. Einstein's universe is obtained if in (D) $R^2$ is replaced by $-\frac{R^2}{c^2}$
and moreover $M$ is set equal to 1, where $R$ denotes the constant (independent of $x_4$)
radius of curvature of space. De Sitter's universe is obtained if in (D) $R^2$ is replaced by
$-\frac{R^2}{c^2}$ and $M$ by $\cos x_1$:

$$d\tau^2 = -\frac{R^2}{c^2}\left(dx_1^2 + \sin^2 x_1\,dx_2^2 + \sin^2 x_1 \sin^2 x_2\,dx_3^2\right) + dx_4^2, \tag{D$_1$}$$

$$d\tau^2 = -\frac{R^2}{c^2}\left(dx_1^2 + \sin^2 x_1\,dx_2^2 + \sin^2 x_1 \sin^2 x_2\,dx_3^2\right) + \cos^2 x_1\,dx_4^2\,{}^{1)}. \tag{D$_2$}$$

4. We must now also make an agreement about the limits within which the world coordinates are
enclosed, i.e. about which points of the four-dimensional manifold are to be regarded as
distinct. Without going into a closer justification, we shall assume that the space
coordinates are enclosed in the following intervals: $x_1$ in the interval $(0, \pi)$; $x_2$ in
the interval $(0, \pi)$ and $x_3$ in the interval $(0, 2\pi)$. With regard to the time
coordinate we make no restrictive assumption for the time being, but shall consider this
question further below.

---

¹) The $ds$, which is assumed to have the dimension of time, we denote by $d\tau$; then the
constant $\varkappa$ has the dimension $\frac{\text{length}}{\text{mass}}$ and in CGS units equals
$1{,}87 \cdot 10^{-27}$. See Laue, *Die Relativitätstheorie*, Vol. II, p. 185.
Braunschweig 1921.

**§ 2.** 1. From the assumptions (C) and (D) it follows, if one sets $i = 1, 2, 3$ and $k = 4$
in the equations (A):

$$R'(x_4)\frac{\partial M}{\partial x_1} = R'(x_4)\frac{\partial M}{\partial x_2} = R'(x_4)\frac{\partial M}{\partial x_3} = 0;$$

from this two cases result: (1.) $R'(x_4) = 0$, $R$ is independent of $x_4$; we shall call this
world the *stationary world*; (2.) $R'(x_4)$ not $= 0$, $M$ depends only on $x_4$; this is to be
called the *non-stationary world*.

We first consider the stationary world and write down the equations (A) for
$i, k = 1, 2, 3$ and moreover $i$ not $= k$; we then obtain the following system of formulas:

$$\frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_1\,\partial x_2} - \operatorname{cotg} x_1 \frac{\partial M}{\partial x_2} = 0,$$
$$\frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_1\,\partial x_3} - \operatorname{cotg} x_1 \frac{\partial M}{\partial x_3} = 0,$$
$$\frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_2\,\partial x_3} - \operatorname{cotg} x_2 \frac{\partial M}{\partial x_3} = 0.$$

The integration of these equations yields the following expression for $M$:

$$M = A(x_3, x_4)\sin x_1 \sin x_2 + B(x_2, x_4)\sin x_1 + C(x_1, x_4), \tag{1}$$

where $A$, $B$, $C$ are arbitrary functions of their arguments. If we solve the equations (A)
for $R_{ik}$ and eliminate the unknown density $\varrho$¹) from the equations not yet used, then,
on substituting the expression (1) for $M$, we obtain, after somewhat lengthy but quite
elementary calculations, the following two possibilities for $M$:

$$M = M_0 = \text{const}, \tag{2}$$
$$M = (A_0 x_4 + B_0)\cos x_1, \tag{3}$$

where $M_0$, $A_0$, $B_0$ denote constants.

If $M$ equals a constant, the stationary world is the cylindrical world. Here it is convenient
to work with the gravitational potentials of formula (D$_1$); if we determine the density and
the quantity $\lambda$, we obtain Einstein's well-known result:

$$\lambda = \frac{c^2}{R^2}, \qquad \varrho = \frac{2}{\varkappa R^2}, \qquad \overline{M} = \frac{4\pi^2}{\varkappa} R,$$

where $\overline{M}$ denotes the total mass of space.

---

¹) The density $\varrho$ is for us an unknown function of the world coordinates
$x_1, x_2, x_3, x_4$.

[p. 381]

In the second possible case, when $M$ is given by (3), we arrive by means of a reasonable
transformation of $x_4$¹) at de Sitter's spherical world, in which $M = \cos x_1$; with the
help of (D$_2$) we obtain de Sitter's relations:

$$\lambda = \frac{3c^2}{R^2}, \qquad \varrho = 0, \qquad \overline{M} = 0.$$

We thus have the following result: *the stationary world is either Einstein's cylindrical
world or de Sitter's spherical world.*

2. We now want to consider the non-stationary world. $M$ is now a function of $x_4$; by a
suitable choice of $x_4$ one can achieve (without loss of generality) that $M = 1$; in order to
connect with our usual notions, we give $ds^2$ a form analogous to (D$_1$) and (D$_2$):

$$d\tau^2 = -\frac{R^2(x_4)}{c^2}\left(dx_1^2 + \sin^2 x_1\,dx_2^2 + \sin^2 x_1 \sin^2 x_2\,dx_3^2\right) + dx_4^2. \tag{D$_3$}$$

Our task is now the determination of $R$ and $\varrho$ from the equations (A). It is clear that
the equations (A) with different indices give nothing; the equations (A) for
$i = k = 1, 2, 3$ give one relation:

$$\frac{R'^2}{R^2} + \frac{2RR''}{R^2} + \frac{c^2}{R^2} - \lambda = 0, \tag{4}$$

the equation (A) with $i = k = 4$ yields the relation:

$$\frac{3R'^2}{R^2} + \frac{3c^2}{R^2} - \lambda = \varkappa c^2 \varrho, \tag{5}$$

[Translator's note: in the scan the first denominator in (5) is printed with a damaged glyph
that looks like $k^2$; it is $R^2$, as in (4).]

with

$$R' = \frac{dR}{dx_4} \quad\text{and}\quad R'' = \frac{d^2R}{dx_4^2}.$$

Since $R'$ is not $= 0$, the integration of equation (4), if we also write $t$ for $x_4$, gives
the following equation:

$$\frac{1}{c^2}\left(\frac{dR}{dt}\right)^2 = \frac{A - R + \frac{\lambda}{3c^2}R^3}{R}, \tag{6}$$

where $A$ is an arbitrary constant. From this equation we obtain $R$ by inversion of an
elliptic integral, i.e. by solving for $R$ the equation

$$t = \frac{1}{c}\int_a^R \sqrt{\frac{x}{A - x + \frac{\lambda}{3c^2}x^3}}\,dx + B, \tag{7}$$

in which $B$ and $a$ are constants, whereby account must still be taken of the usual
conditions for the change of sign of the square root. From equation (5) $\varrho$ can be
determined as

$$\varrho = \frac{3A}{\varkappa R^3}; \tag{8}$$

the constant $A$ is expressed through the total mass of space $\overline{M}$ as follows:

$$A = \frac{\varkappa \overline{M}}{6\pi^2}. \tag{9}$$

If $\overline{M}$ is positive, then $A$ is also positive.

3. We must base the consideration of the non-stationary world on the equations (6) and (7);
here the quantity $\lambda$ is not determined; we shall assume that it can have arbitrary
values. We now determine those values of the variable $x$ at which the square root of formula
(7) can change its sign. If we restrict our consideration to positive radii of curvature, it
suffices to consider the interval $(0, \infty)$ for $x$, and in this interval the values of $x$
which make the radicand equal to $0$ or $\infty$. One value of $x$ for which the square root in
(7) becomes zero is $x = 0$; the remaining values of $x$ at which the square root in (7)
changes its sign are given by the positive roots of the equation
$A - x + \frac{\lambda}{3c^2}x^3 = 0$. We denote $\frac{\lambda}{3c^2}$ by $y$ and consider in
the $(x, y)$-plane the family of curves of the third degree:

$$y x^3 - x + A = 0. \tag{10}$$

$A$ is here the parameter of the family, which varies in the interval $(0, \infty)$. The curves
of the family (see Fig.) cut the $x$-axis at the point $x = A$, $y = 0$ and have a maximum at

$$x = \frac{3A}{2}, \qquad y = \frac{4}{27A^2}.$$

From the figure it is evident that for negative $\lambda$ the equation
$A - x + \frac{\lambda}{3c^2}x^3 = 0$ has one positive root $x_0$ in the interval $(0, A)$.
If $x_0$ is regarded as a function of $\lambda$ and $A$:

$$x_0 = \Theta(\lambda, A),$$

one finds that $\Theta$ is an increasing function of $\lambda$ and an increasing function of
$A$. If $\lambda$ lies in the interval $\left(0, \frac{4}{9}\frac{c^2}{A^2}\right)$, the equation
has two positive roots $x_0 = \Theta(\lambda, A)$ and $x_0' = \vartheta(\lambda, A)$, where $x_0$
falls in the interval $\left(A, \frac{3A}{2}\right)$ and $x_0'$ in the interval
$\left(\frac{3A}{2}, \infty\right)$; $\Theta(\lambda, A)$ is an increasing function of $\lambda$
as well as of $A$, and $\vartheta(\lambda, A)$ a decreasing function of $\lambda$ and $A$.
Finally, if $\lambda$ is greater than $\frac{4}{9}\frac{c^2}{A^2}$, the equation has no positive
roots.

We now pass to the consideration of formula (7), and preface it with the following remark:
let the radius of curvature at $t = t_0$ equal $R_0$; the sign of the square root in (7) is
positive or negative for $t = t_0$ according as the radius of curvature is increasing or
decreasing at $t = t_0$; by replacing $t$ by $-t$ if necessary, we can always make the square
root positive, i.e. by the choice of time one can always arrange that the radius of curvature
increases with increasing time at $t = t_0$.

4. We first consider the case $\lambda > \frac{4}{9}\frac{c^2}{A^2}$, i.e. the case in which the
equation $A - x + \frac{\lambda}{3c^2}x^3 = 0$ has no positive roots. Equation (7) can then be
written as

$$t - t_0 = \frac{1}{c}\int_{R_0}^{R} \sqrt{\frac{x}{A - x + \frac{\lambda}{3c^2}x^3}}\,dx, \tag{11}$$

where, by our remark, the square root is always positive. It follows that *$R$ is an
increasing function of $t$*; the positive initial value $R_0$ is free of any restriction.

Since the radius of curvature cannot be smaller than zero, it must, as the time $t$
decreases, decrease from $R_0$ and reach the value zero at an instant $t'$. The time of growth
of $R$

[p. 384]

from $0$ to $R_0$ we shall call the *time since the creation of the world*¹); this time $t'$ is
given by:

$$t' = \frac{1}{c}\int_0^{R_0} \sqrt{\frac{x}{A - x + \frac{\lambda}{3c^2}x^3}}\,dx. \tag{12}$$

The world under consideration we call a *monotonic world of the first kind*.

The time since the creation of the (monotonic) world (of the first kind), regarded as a
function of $R_0$, $A$, $\lambda$, has the following properties: 1. it grows with growing $R_0$;
2. it decreases when $A$ increases, i.e. when the mass in space increases; 3. it decreases
when $\lambda$ increases. If $A > \frac{2}{3}R_0$, then for any $\lambda$ the time elapsed since
the creation of the world is finite; if $A \leqq \frac{2}{3}R_0$, then a value
$\lambda = \lambda_1 = \frac{4c^2}{9A^2}$ can always be found such that, as $\lambda$ approaches
this value, the time since the creation of the world increases without bound.

5. Now let $\lambda$ lie in the interval $\left(0, \frac{4c^2}{9A^2}\right)$; then the initial
value of the radius of curvature can lie in the intervals: $(0, x_0)$, $(x_0, x_0')$,
$(x_0', \infty)$. If $R_0$ falls in the interval $(x_0, x_0')$, the square root in formula (7)
is imaginary; a space with this initial curvature is impossible.

To the case where $R_0$ lies in the interval $(0, x_0)$ we devote the next section; here we
consider the third case: $R_0 > x_0'$ or $R_0 > \vartheta(\lambda, A)$. By considerations
analogous to the preceding ones one can show that $R$ is an *increasing* function of time,
where $R$ can begin with the value $x_0' = \vartheta(\lambda, A)$. The time that has elapsed
from the instant when $R = x_0'$ to the instant corresponding to $R = R_0$ we again call the
time since the creation of the world. Let it be $t'$; then

$$t' = \frac{1}{c}\int_{x_0'}^{R_0} \sqrt{\frac{x}{A - x + \frac{\lambda}{3c^2}x^3}}\,dx. \tag{13}$$

This world we call a *monotonic world of the second kind*.

¹) The time since the creation of the world is the time that has elapsed from the instant when

6. We now consider the case where $\lambda$ falls between the limits $(-\infty, 0)$. If in this
case $R_0 > x_0 = \Theta(\lambda, A)$, the square root in (7) becomes imaginary, and the space
with this $R_0$ is impossible. If $R_0 < x_0$, the case considered is identical with the one we
set aside in the previous section. We therefore assume that $\lambda$ lies in the interval
$\left(-\infty, \frac{4c^2}{9A^2}\right)$ and that $R_0 < x_0$. By well-known
considerations¹) one can now show that $R$ becomes a periodic function of $t$, with the period
$t_\pi$, which we call the *world period*; $t_\pi$ is given by the formula:

$$t_\pi = \frac{2}{c}\int_0^{x_0} \sqrt{\frac{x}{A - x + \frac{\lambda}{3c^2}x^3}}\,dx. \tag{14}$$

The radius of curvature varies here between $0$ and $x_0$. We shall call this world the
*periodic world*. The period of the periodic world increases when we increase $\lambda$, and
tends to infinity when $\lambda$ tends to the value $\lambda_1 = \frac{4c^2}{9A^2}$.

For small $\lambda$ the period is represented by the approximate formula

$$t_\pi = \frac{\pi A}{c}. \tag{15}$$

With regard to the periodic world two points of view are possible: if we count two events as
coinciding when their space coordinates coincide and the difference of their time coordinates
is an integer multiple of the period, then the radius of curvature grows from $0$ to $x_0$ and
then decreases to the value $0$; the time of existence of the world is finite. On the other
hand, if time varies between $-\infty$ and $+\infty$ (i.e. we regard two events as coinciding
only if not only their space coordinates but also their time coordinates coincide), we arrive
at a genuine periodicity of the curvature of space.

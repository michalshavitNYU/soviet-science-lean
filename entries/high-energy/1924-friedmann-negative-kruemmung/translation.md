**On the Possibility of a World with Constant Negative Curvature of Space**

By **A. Friedmann** in Petersburg.

(Received 7 January 1924.)

**§ 1.** 1. In our note "On the curvature of space"¹) we considered those solutions of
Einstein's world equations which lead to types of world having a constant positive curvature as
a common feature; there we discussed all possible cases. The possibility of deriving from the
world equations a world of constant positive spatial curvature is, however, connected with the
question of the finiteness of space. For this reason it may be of interest to investigate
whether one can obtain from the same world equations a world of constant negative curvature,
of whose finiteness (even under some supplementary assumptions) there can hardly be any
question.

In the present note it is shown that it is indeed possible to derive from Einstein's world
equations a world with constant negative curvature of space. As in the cited paper, we have to
distinguish two cases here too, namely 1. the case of a stationary world, whose curvature is
constant in time, and 2. the case of a non-stationary world, whose curvature is constant in
space but varies in the course of time. Between the stationary worlds of constant negative and
those of constant positive spatial curvature there is an essential difference. The worlds of
stationary negative curvature, namely, admit no positive density of matter; the density is
either zero or negative. The physically possible stationary worlds (i.e. those with
non-negative density of matter) therefore have their analogue in de Sitter's world, but not in
Einstein's world²).

At the end of this note we shall touch on the question whether one may at all judge the
finiteness or infiniteness of space on the basis of its curvature.

2. We turn to our general assumptions, which we think of as grouped into the same two classes
as in the cited note;

in doing so we keep our earlier notation. The assumptions of the first class consist in taking
as Einstein's world equations the equations (A), (B), (C) of the paper mentioned. The
assumptions of the second class will now differ from the earlier ones. If we assume that one
of the world coordinates, $x_4$, may be called the time coordinate, we can express the
assumptions of the second class (for the case under consideration, the world with negative
constant curvature of space) by requiring that the interval $ds$ be of the form

$$ds^2 = \frac{R^2\,(dx_1^2 + dx_2^2 + dx_3^2)}{x_3^2} + M^2\,dx_4^2, \tag{D$'$}$$

where $R$ is a function of time and $M$ a function of all four world coordinates. The constant
negative curvature of the space of our world is then proportional to $-\frac{1}{R^2}$¹).

Taking into account that for our world $ds^2$ is an indefinite form, we can, with a change of
notation, rewrite the formula (D′) as follows:

$$d\tau^2 = -\frac{R^2}{c^2}\,\frac{(dx_1^2 + dx_2^2 + dx_3^2)}{x_3^2} + M^2\,dx_4^2. \tag{D$''$}$$

Of course the spatial curvature of our world remains negative and proportional to
$-\frac{1}{R^2}$.

The problem before us consists in finding two functions $R$ and $M$ which are to satisfy
Einstein's world equations, i.e. the equations (A), (B) and (C) of the note mentioned.

Setting $i = 1, 2, 3$, $k = 4$ in (A), we obtain the following three equations:

$$R'(x_4)\,\frac{\partial M}{\partial x_1} = R'(x_4)\,\frac{\partial M}{\partial x_2}
= R'(x_4)\,\frac{\partial M}{\partial x_3} = 0.$$

These equations show that the worlds under consideration can belong to one of two types:

1st type. Stationary worlds, $R' = 0$, $R$ is constant in time.

2nd type. Non-stationary worlds, $R' \neq 0$, $M$ depends only on time.

We first consider the case of the stationary world; the case of the non-stationary world shows
a great similarity with that of the non-stationary world of constant positive spatial
curvature; for this reason we shall treat this second case only quite briefly.

**§ 2.** 1. The equations (A) give, for the indices $i, k = 1, 2, 3$:

$$\frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_1\,\partial x_2} = 0,$$

$$\frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_2\,\partial x_3} + \frac{1}{x_3}\,\frac{\partial M}{\partial x_2} = 0, \qquad
\frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_1\,\partial x_3} + \frac{1}{x_3}\,\frac{\partial M}{\partial x_1} = 0.$$

The integration of these equations gives:

$$M = \frac{P(x_1, x_4) + Q(x_2, x_4)}{x_3} + L(x_3, x_4), \tag{1}$$

where $P$, $Q$ and $L$ are, for the time being, arbitrary functions of their arguments.

To determine $P$, $Q$ and $L$ we use the equations (A), in which one has to set
$i, k = 1, 2, 3$.

The calculation gives:

$$\left.\begin{aligned}
-\frac{1}{M}\left(\frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_2^2} + \frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_3^2}\right) &= \frac{1 - \lambda R^2}{x_3^2},\\
-\frac{1}{M}\left(\frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_3^2}\right) &= \frac{1 - \lambda R^2}{x_3^2},\\
-\frac{1}{M}\left(\frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_1^2} + \frac{\partial^2 M}{\partial x_2^2}\right) + \frac{2}{x_3}\,\frac{1}{M}\,\frac{\partial M}{\partial x_3} &= \frac{1 - \lambda R^2}{x_3^2}.
\end{aligned}\right\} \tag{2}$$

Subtracting the first equation of this system from the second, one obtains:

$$\frac{\partial^2 P}{\partial x_1^2} = \frac{\partial^2 Q}{\partial x_2^2}.$$

From this it follows:

$$\left.\begin{aligned}
P &= n(x_4)\,x_1^2 + a_1(x_4) + b_1(x_4),\\
Q &= n(x_4)\,x_2^2 + a_2(x_4) + b_2(x_4).
\end{aligned}\right\} \tag{3}$$

Taking (1) and (3) into account, the last of the equations (2) can be written in the form

$$-\frac{3 - \lambda R^2}{x_3^3}\,(P + Q) = \frac{4n}{x_3} + \frac{1 - \lambda R^2}{x_3^2}
- \frac{2}{x_3}\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_3}. \tag{4}$$

If, therefore, $P + Q$ actually contains one of the quantities $x_1$ or $x_2$, i.e. if one of
the coefficients $n$, $a_1$, $a_2$ is different from zero, the factor of $P + Q$ in the equation
(4) must

vanish; for the right-hand side of this equation depends neither on $x_1$ nor on $x_2$. The case

In the case that the quantities $n, a_1, a_2$ do not all three vanish, there must therefore hold
between $\lambda$ and the curvature of space the relation

$$\lambda R^3 = 3 \tag{5}$$

Taking Eq. (5) into account, the equations (2) reduce to a single equation, which determines the

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_3} + \frac{L}{x_3} = 2n. \tag{6}$$

2. In what follows we must distinguish two cases: 1. $n \neq 0$, 2. $n = 0$. In the first case
the formulas (D′), (1) and (3) show us that the quantity $n$ may be taken equal to 1 without
loss of generality; namely, by a suitable substitution $x_4 = \varphi(x_4)$ one can always
achieve $n = 1$. Taking this into account, Eq. (6) gives:

$$L = \frac{L_0(x_4)}{x_3} + x_3. \tag{7}$$

To determine $\varrho$ we set $i = k = 4$ in the equations (A); a simple calculation shows that
in our case $\varrho$ becomes equal to zero. The first case is therefore characterized by the
vanishing density of matter and by the interval:

$$ds^2 = \frac{R^2}{x_3^2}\,(dx_1^2 + dx_2^2 + dx_3^2)
+ \left(\frac{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + a_1(x_4)\,x_1 + a_2(x_4)\,x_2 + a_3(x_4) + x_3^2}{x_3}\right)^2 dx_4^2 \tag{D$'_1$}$$

Passing to the second case ($n = 0$), we find for $L$ the equation:

$$L = \frac{L_0(x_4)}{x_3}. \tag{8}$$

In this case too the calculation gives the value zero for $\varrho$. Thus the second case is
likewise characterized by the vanishing density of matter and by the interval:

$$ds^2 = \frac{R}{x_3^2}\,(dx_1^2 + dx_2^2 + dx_3^2)
+ \left[\frac{a_1(x_4)\,x_1 + a_2(x_4)\,x_2 + a_3(x_4)}{x_3}\right]^2 dx_4^2. \tag{D$'_2$}$$

[Translator's note: the first factor is printed $\frac{R}{x_3^2}$; by (D′) it should be
$\frac{R^2}{x_3^2}$.]

Finally we consider the case that all three coefficients $n, a_1, a_2$ vanish, so that $M$ does
not depend on $x_1$ and $x_2$.

In the integration of Eq. (2) we again come upon two cases:

$$1.\ \lambda R^2 = 3, \qquad M = \frac{M_0(x_4)}{x_3},$$

$$2.\ \lambda R^2 = 1, \qquad M = M(x_4),$$

where $M_0$ and $M$ are arbitrary functions of their arguments.

The first case turns out to be a special case of the interval determined by the formula
(D′₂); an easy calculation shows that the density $\varrho$ of matter vanishes here.

The second case¹), as one easily convinces oneself, leads to a density of matter different from
zero. To decide whether the density here turns out positive or negative, we must use that form
of the interval which corresponds to an indefinite quadratic form and is expressed by the
formula (D″). Calculating with the gravitational potentials of the formula (D″), one finds that
in the case under consideration $M$ is a function of $x_4$ alone; consequently we can, without
loss of generality, set $M = 1$ [for this one need only introduce the coordinate
$\overline{x}_4 = \varphi(x_4)$ in place of $x_4$]. Calculating the density $\varrho$ under this
assumption, one finds:

$$\lambda = -\frac{c^2}{R^2}, \qquad \varrho = -\frac{2}{\varkappa R^2},$$

$$d\tau^2 = -\frac{R^2}{c^2}\,\frac{dx_1^2 + dx_2^2 + dx_3^2}{x_3^2} + dx_4^2. \tag{D$''_3$}$$

This case therefore gives a negative value for $\varrho$.

Summing up, we can say that **the stationary world with constant negative curvature of space is
possible only with vanishing or negative density of matter; the interval corresponding to this
world is expressed by the formulas (D′₁), (D′₂) and (D″₃) given above.**

3. We now turn to the case of the non-stationary world. We first remark that here $M$ is a
function of $x_4$ alone; the considerations we have used several times show that one may take
$M$ equal to unity. Under these assumptions we find without difficulty that the equations (A)
for $i = 1, 2, 3$; $k = 4$; and for $i, k = 1, 2, 3$ [translator's note: presumably $i \neq k$, since
the next sentence uses $i = k$] are satisfied identically. Setting here $i = k = 1, 2, 3$, we obtain the
second-order differential equation that serves to determine the function $R(x_4)$, namely:

$$\frac{R'^2}{R^2} + \frac{2RR''}{R^2} + \frac{1}{R^2} - \lambda = 0. \tag{9}$$

This equation is completely analogous to our earlier equation [Eq. (4) of the cited note]; the
latter goes over exactly into Eq. (9) if one sets $c = 1$ there. We can therefore transfer the
whole discussion of Eq. (4), l. c., to the equation just written down. Accordingly we shall not
go into it, but only compute the density of matter $\varrho$ for the non-stationary world.

If, for the case of the non-stationary world, we write the interval in the form (D″), we obtain
for $R$ the differential equation:

$$\frac{R'^2}{R^2} + \frac{2RR''}{R^2} - \frac{c^2}{R^2} - \lambda = 0.$$

The integration of this equation gives us the relation:

$$\frac{R'^2}{c^2} = \frac{A + R + \frac{\lambda}{3c^2}\,R^3}{R},$$

where $A$ is an arbitrary constant. Computing the density $\varrho$, one obtains:

$$\varrho = \frac{3A}{\varkappa R^3}. \tag{10}$$

The formula (10) shows that for a positive $A$ the density of matter is likewise positive.

**From this follows the possibility of non-stationary worlds with constant negative curvature of
space and with positive density of matter.**

**§ 3.** 1. We turn to the discussion of the physical meaning of the result obtained in the
preceding paragraphs. We have convinced ourselves that Einstein's world equations possess
solutions which correspond to a world with constant negative curvature of space. This fact
indicates that the world equations taken by themselves are not yet sufficient to decide the
question of the finiteness of our world. Knowledge of the curvature of space does not yet give us
any direct indication of its finiteness or infiniteness. To reach a definite conclusion about the
finiteness of space, one needs some supplementary agreements. Indeed, we call a space finite if
the distance between any two non-coinciding points of this space does not exceed a certain
constant number, whatever pairs of points we may take. Consequently, before we attack the
problem of the finiteness of space, we must still agree which points of this space we regard as
distinct. If, for example, we regard a sphere as a surface in three-dimensional Euclidean space,

[p. 332]

then we count the points which lie on the same circle of latitude and whose longitudes differ by
exactly 360° as coinciding; if, on the other hand, we had regarded these points as distinct, we
would obtain a many-sheeted spherical surface in Euclidean space. The distance between any two
points on a sphere does not exceed a finite number; if, however, we regard this sphere as an
infinitely-many-sheeted surface, we can (by assigning the points to different sheets in a
suitable way) make this distance arbitrarily large. From this it is clear that, before entering
into considerations about the finiteness of space, one must specify which points are regarded
as coinciding and which as distinct.

2. As a criterion for the distinctness of points one could use, among others, the principle of
"Phantomenangst" [fear of phantoms]. By this we mean the axiom that between any two distinct
points one can draw only one straight (geodesic) line. If one adopts this principle, then two
points that can be joined by more than one straight line may not be regarded as distinct.
According to this principle, for example, the two endpoints of the same diameter of a sphere are
not distinct from each other. Of course this principle excludes the possibility of phantoms, for
the phantom appears at the same point as the image that produces it.

The formulation of the concept of coinciding and non-coinciding points just discussed may well
lead to the idea that spaces with positive constant curvature are finite. However, the
criterion mentioned does not allow us to conclude that spaces of negative constant curvature are
finite. This is the reason why, in our opinion, Einstein's world equations without supplementary
assumptions are not yet sufficient to draw a conclusion about the finiteness of our world.

St. Petersburg, November 1923.
